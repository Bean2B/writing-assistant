# Cultivation fiction

Ask what becoming an immortal means to this particular being. A mortal may want one more winter with someone. A river spirit may want to keep its name when the river is diverted. Let the first success change what the protagonist wants, what others now expect and what walking away would cost. A bond, debt, affection or moral limit can pull a reluctant person into a contest.

By the end of the opening volume, let the reader recognize something the protagonist can now do and something still beyond reach. Show the difference under changed conditions. Allow partial success, refusal, loss or an opportunity that simply passes. Let recurring characters remember the cost differently.

For worlds drawing on historical China, decide whose word counts in a household, guild or sect, who can travel or inherit, and where cultivation changes those rules. Research terms for the chosen period and locality when uncertain. Individual motives, power and circumstance shape how gender and rank are experienced.

Carry this method into other genres by changing the object of desire and the institutions that constrain it. Choose a philosophical tension suited to the characters. Let choices, consequences and changes of mind expose it. Leave readers free to side with someone else.
